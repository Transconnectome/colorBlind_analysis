\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

%==============================================================================
% Intro-1: What CVD is
%==============================================================================
Color vision deficiency (CVD) affects approximately 8\% of men and 0.4\% of women of European ancestry~\citep{birch2012}.
It arises from altered spectral sensitivity of the L- or M-cone photopigment. This altered sensitivity is driven by X-linked polymorphisms in the cone opsin genes~\citep{deeb2005, neitz2011}. In anomalous trichromats, the L- and M-cone spectral peaks are separated by only about 2--12~nm, versus roughly 25~nm in normal trichromacy~\citep{neitz2011}. This reduced separation weakens the L--M cone-opponent signal rather than abolishing it.

A weakened L--M signal yields a characteristic peripheral phenotype. Affected observers show reduced red--green discrimination, frequent category confusions, and elevated just-noticeable differences along the L--M chromatic axis. The Ishihara test screens for the resulting red--green confusions~\citep{ishihara1917}. The elevated discrimination thresholds are quantified instead by threshold psychophysics~\citep{boehm2014, bosten2019}.

%==============================================================================
% Intro-2: Current CVD correction and its limits
%==============================================================================
Current CVD correction methods fall into two structurally related families. Hardware filters form the first family. Dichroic notch lenses, such as the EnChroma line, are designed to filter narrow spectral bands and amplify the residual red--green signal. Software filters form the second. Brettel--Vi\'enot--Mollon simulation~\citep{brettel1997} and the parameterized Machado retinal model~\citep{machado2009} forward-project a stimulus onto an anomalous retina. Their Daltonization inverses, including recent algorithmic reformulations~\citep{akalin2025}, redistribute the lost contrast into channels the user can still see. Across this diversity, the two families share one commitment: they optimize against a population-average retina and apply the same transform to every user.

Attempts to escape this average-user ceiling have mostly taken one route: tuning a retinal cone-shift parameter to a user's responses on plate-style or appearance-matching tests. The criterion is then the appearance \emph{report}, not the cortical representation that downstream tasks read out, so these methods personalize at the retinal rather than the cortical level. Their results show the cost. Improvements do not generalize, because the phenotype varies markedly even within one diagnostic category~\citep{bosten2019}. Color-enhancing glasses improved discrimination for only one of two consumer products tested~\citep{patterson2022}, and population-average filters shift the appearance of color while leaving generalized color discrimination largely unchanged~\citep{somers2024}. What is missing is a filter derived from, and grounded in, the user's \emph{own} neural color representation.

%==============================================================================
% Intro-3: Neural features of CVD
%==============================================================================
CVD is not an on--off loss of color. The downstream cortical color machinery stays intact. It receives an input that is quantitatively reweighted but qualitatively similar to a normal input. The cortical color representation is therefore not absent but structurally distorted. These features make the cortical representation the natural level at which to examine the distortion.

Cortical color is a distributed, hierarchical computation~\citep{gegenfurtner2003, shapley2011, conway2018}. Early visual cortex carries cone-opponent signals. Higher-order regions, in particular hV4, support a population code for hue~\citep{brouwer2009}, and act as a hub linking seen and remembered color~\citep{bannert2018}. Continuous hue can be reconstructed from these distributed patterns~\citep{brouwer2009}, and hue-selective responses to intermediate colors appear as early as V1~\citep{parkes2009, kuriki2015}. This code is shared enough across people that one observer's color responses can be read out in a common space, an approach recently validated in healthy observers~\citep{bannert2025}.

Anomalous trichromats do more than lose sensitivity. They also compensate. Long-term cortical adaptation reshapes hue appearance independently of the retina~\citep{boehm2014, webster2015, tregillus2021}. In deutan observers, chromatic-contrast adaptation exceeds what threshold sensitivity loss predicts, consistent with a post-receptoral gain that partly offsets the weaker L--M signal~\citep{basim2025}. Chromatic responses also realign toward the blue--yellow direction in these observers~\citep{emery2021}. Because this evidence comes from a separate paradigm, it motivates rather than establishes a cortical locus, and we do not replicate it here. Together these findings locate key variance at the retinal--cortical interface, not at the retina alone.

Because this compensation lives in cortex, a correction calibrated to the retina or to appearance reports inherits it and acts at the wrong level. We therefore take the individual's cortical readout, not the simulated retina, as the reference for correction. This is a design hypothesis, and the rest of the paper tests it.

%==============================================================================
% Intro-4: Gaps
%==============================================================================
Three concrete gaps remain. \emph{(Gap 1)} Neural studies of color describe geometry at the group level in healthy observers~\citep{brouwer2009, kuriki2015, bannert2018}. The cortical color geometry of an individual CVD observer, the personal trait on which a tailored correction would be built, has not been characterized. \emph{(Gap 2)} The measures applied to CVD do not capture this geometry. fMRI in CVD has quantified response magnitude: contrast-response gain in anomalous trichromacy~\citep{tregillus2021}, and activation in dichromacy and achromatopsia~\citep{rina2024}. Where multivoxel patterns have been analyzed, the readout has been classification accuracy. Magnitude indexes signal strength and accuracy indexes category identity, but neither preserves the continuous relational structure~\citep{brouwer2013} that a hue-space filter must reshape. \emph{(Gap 3)} Across studies that analyze multivoxel color patterns, we are not aware of a corrective filter inverted from an individual's own cortical representation. Whether such a high-dimensional signal can be turned into a realizable, individualized correction is untested.

%==============================================================================
% Intro-5: Research questions and hypotheses
%==============================================================================
We ask whether an individual's cortical color geometry can serve as the reference for an individualized correction. To answer this, we characterize two adults with CVD, one with each major red--green subtype, against a reference group of healthy controls (see Methods).

A stimulus-space filter is both warranted and tractable only in a specific regime. The displayed colors must stay neurally distinguishable, or the filter has no signal to act on. The continuous arrangement of hues must also be distorted, or there is nothing to correct. Our questions follow this logic.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Describe.} Does an individual's cortical hue geometry in CVD carry structured distortion, with categorical discrimination preserved but the continuous arrangement of hues impaired?
\item \textbf{Summarize.} Can that distortion be captured by a few interpretable parameters, treated as a compact description rather than as physiological measurements?
\item \textbf{Correct.} Can a stimulus-space correction be derived from those parameters as a realizable, individualized filter?
\item \textbf{Validate.} Does the filter move the cortical representation toward the healthy-control reference, more so than a deployed accessibility filter?
\end{enumerate}

We expect the continuous arrangement of hues to be selectively impaired while categorical discrimination is preserved. A few interpretable parameters should capture this distortion, and inverting them should yield a realizable, individualized correction.

Together, these steps yield a filter built for one individual. Its input is the cortical signal, its parameters are the fitted description, and its output is a stimulus shift. Each is defined at the level of the single observer, the level at which any correction must ultimately act. Because the sample holds one participant per subtype, this study tests the feasibility of the inversion: whether an individual's cortical color geometry can be turned into a realizable filter. It does not test whether per-person correction outperforms a subtype average, which would require several individuals within one subtype.
