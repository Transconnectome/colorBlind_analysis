\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

We asked whether the cortical color representation of a color-vision-deficient (CVD) individual is structured enough to ground a personalized corrective filter.
Two CVD participants, one deutan and one protan, retained categorical discrimination of all eight color hues at every retinotopic ROI.
Yet both selectively lost continuous-hue interpolation, falling at or below the adjacent-accuracy chance level.
This deficit was not diffuse signal loss but a structured geometric distortion.
We modelled it with two cortical components: a rotation along the subtype confusion axis --- the classical direction of CVD color confusion --- and a distortion along the S-cone axis, where the interpolation deficit was concentrated.
Fit independently per participant from behavioral discrimination thresholds and cortical representational geometry, this model recovered an exact stimulus-space pre-image for all eight hues in both cases.
That pre-image is the per-person correction filter, built from that individual's own cortical color geometry.
We propose that such a filter pulls the distorted representation back toward the healthy-control geometry, improving both behavioral hue discrimination and the cortical color response.

\subsection{A geometric distortion of cortical color representation}
We identify that CVD is a structured distortion of cortical color geometry rather than a uniform attenuation of the chromatic signal.
Representational geometry treats the pairwise distance structure among stimuli as an encoded relationship in each cortical region \cite{kriegeskorte2008, kriegeskorte2019}.
Two complementary measurements expose a structural distortion, each at the stage where its signal exists.
The representational dissimilarity matrix (RDM) shows \emph{which} pairwise distances deviate from the healthy-control (HC) geometry: disparity was significantly elevated at V2 in the deutan participant (Crawford \& Howell $p = 0.040$) and at V1 in the protan participant ($p = 0.007$).
This subject-specific ROI pattern is inconsistent with a shared group-level gain.
Leave-one-color-out decoding shows \emph{where} the continuous-hue manifold breaks down.
Only hV4 supported above-chance interpolation in HC ($0.47 \pm 0.05$, $p = 0.044$), replicating \citeA{brouwer2009}.
There, the protan participant fell significantly below HC ($0.13$, $p = 0.024$; exploratory single-case), and the deutan participant showed a marginal reduction in the same direction ($0.25$, $p = 0.082$).
Both sat at zero adjacent accuracy, with a significant deficit at the S-cone intermediate blue ($d = 2.20$, $p = 0.042$).
This account aligns with prior characterizations of CVD as a multidimensional deformation of color space rather than a uniform attenuation \cite{boehm2014, emery2021}.

\subsection{A neurally grounded, individualizable correction filter}
We built each correction filter by inverting that participant's fitted two-component model to its stimulus-space pre-image (Methods~\S\ref{sec:methods:filter}) --- the displayed hue that, after the cortex distorts it, is perceived as the intended color.
Across the eight hues this filter applied a mean rotation of $|\delta\theta| = 26.3^\circ$ for the deutan participant and $16.2^\circ$ for the protan participant.

Fitting the behavioral and neural loss atoms independently revealed three benefits of grounding the correction in cortical geometry.
First, the neural term recovered a confusion-axis direction the behavioral loss could not resolve: the protan behavioral-only fit did not beat the no-correction baseline ($\Delta L = +0.01$, 4 of 7 folds), whereas the RDM term resolved a clear rotation.
Second, it stabilized an already-informative solution: for the deutan participant the behavioral-only and combined fits shared the same argmin $(6^\circ, -42^\circ)$, and adding the neural term sharpened it without shifting it, lowering the boundary-saturation rate from $23\%$ to $9.3\%$.
Third, it tightened parameter stability in both participants, narrowing the resample interquartile range of the recovered parameters.
The neural-only fit for the deutan participant independently returned the same direction ($\hat\beta_c = -26^\circ$), corroborating the behavioral estimate.
A correction grounded in individual cortical geometry therefore differs in substance from a purely behavioral one.

The resulting filter is per-person and neurally grounded --- the architectural advance over a population-average correction.
The two fitted filters differ by subtype in the \emph{direction} of the dominant confusion-axis term ($\hat\beta_c = -42^\circ$ for the deutan participant versus $+24^\circ$ for the protan participant).
Per-axis magnitude is not identifiable (below) and is not the basis of the contrast.
The architecture is individualizable by construction: each filter is built from one participant's measured cortical color geometry rather than from a fixed subtype-average spectral shift.
Because $\theta_{\rm conf}$ is subtype-defined and the sample holds one deutan and one protan, this contrast is between-subtype.
What makes each correction individual is that its distortion is read from that participant's own cortical color geometry, not assigned from their diagnostic subtype.
This neural grounding is robust at the level the filter relies on: the two-component mechanism class and the direction (sign) of the dominant confusion-axis term are stable across held-out reference sets.
Their per-axis magnitudes are not separately identifiable (0 of 6 recovery checks survive FDR correction), so the between-subtype contrast rests on these robust directions rather than on absolute parameter values.

A population-average transform cannot substitute for this per-person inverse, for a reason that holds independent of any fit.
The Machado class --- a fixed spectral shift along the single confusion axis, even when scaled by a cortical gain \cite{machado2009} --- stays confined to that one axis and cannot represent distortion in orthogonal directions.
For the protan participant it even collapses three distinct displayed hues onto a single pre-image near $127^\circ$, leaving no exact pre-image where that participant is most vulnerable (Appendix~\ref{app:altmodels}).
Fitting the class confirmed the mismatch empirically --- its gain saturated near $g \approx 3$, implausibly implying overcompensation in observers who remain measurably impaired --- but the structural limit alone rules it out as an exact correction (Section~\ref{sec:methods:candidates}).

\subsection{Testing whether the filter improves perception}
Closing the loop from neural model to perceptual benefit, this study tests whether the personalized filter improves perception in a preregistered two-alternative forced-choice experiment.

We state the prediction in both directions.
If the personalized filter lowers the just-noticeable difference at these hue pairs where the population-average filter does not, individualized cortical-geometry correction is a deployable alternative to population-average approaches.
If the population-average filter instead matches or exceeds it, the cortical-distortion account that motivates the personalized design requires revision.
Either outcome is informative, and the contrast makes the account falsifiable rather than merely illustrative.

\subsection{Limitations}
Four considerations bound the proof-of-concept scope of these findings.
First, the CVD sample is $N = 2$.
Single-case statistics \cite{crawford1998} permit individual-level inference, but population-level claims about deutan or protan subtypes require replication with more participants.
As established above, the sign of $\hat\beta_c$ is the recoverable quantity.
Whether the between-subtype $\hat\beta_c$ difference ($-42^\circ$ vs.\ $+24^\circ$) is subtype-level or idiosyncratic awaits power-adequate replication.
Establishing per-person rather than subtype-average correction specifically requires testing several individuals \emph{within} a single subtype --- the decisive follow-up experiment.
Second, the stimulus set was confined to a single isoluminant, iso-chroma locus ($L^{*} = 75$, chroma $= 40$).
The filter is therefore derived and, in Phase~3, validated only along the hue circle at fixed lightness and saturation.
It does not constrain how the correction generalises to luminance- or saturation-varying stimuli or to naturalistic viewing --- gaps that denser stimulus sampling across these axes would close.
Third, the effective HC pool at hV4 ($n = 6$, after excluding sub-07 for low voxel count) is small enough that the HC $\|\hat\beta\|$ leave-one-out distribution we report is a magnitude anchor for the CVD estimates, not a hypothesis test.
A larger HC reference cohort would be required to arbitrate population-level distortion patterns.
Fourth, we did not compute parameter-level bootstrap confidence intervals on $\hat\beta_s$ and $\hat\beta_c$, so the per-axis estimates are point values whose interval precision awaits the additional runs per participant that bootstrap resampling would require.

\subsection{Conclusion}
This work is a proof-of-concept that each individual's cortical color geometry, measured with fMRI, can ground a stimulus-space correction obtained by inverting a fitted model of that individual's representational distortion.
It recasts what a color-vision deficiency is: not a uniform retinal loss to be undone by a fixed subtype-average spectral shift, but an individually patterned, multidimensional distortion of the cortical color representation --- one structured enough to invert.
The architecture that follows --- measure an individual's cortical geometry, fit its distortion, and invert it to a per-person correction --- is distinct by construction from population-average retinal approaches.
A fixed subtype-average spectral shift cannot represent a per-person distortion, whereas a per-person inverse can, exactly for the mechanism class and up to the per-axis magnitude uncertainty quantified above.

A correction spanning hue, lightness, and chroma jointly --- fitted from denser stimulus sampling, more runs per participant, and a larger HC reference pool --- is the path toward a deployable personalized filter.
More broadly, treating a sensory deficit as an invertible distortion of its own cortical representation --- characterized and corrected one individual at a time --- offers a template for personalized, brain-based perceptual correction beyond color vision.
