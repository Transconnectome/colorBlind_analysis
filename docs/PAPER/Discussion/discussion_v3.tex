\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

We asked whether the cortical color representation of a color-vision-deficient (CVD) individual is structured enough to ground a personalized corrective filter. Two CVD participants, one deutan and one protan, retained categorical discrimination of all eight color hues at every retinotopic ROI. Yet both selectively lost continuous-hue interpolation, falling at or below the adjacent-accuracy chance level. This deficit was not diffuse signal loss but a structured geometric distortion. We modeled it with two cortical components: a rotation along the subtype confusion axis --- the classical direction of CVD color confusion --- and a distortion along the S-cone axis, where the interpolation deficit was concentrated. Fit independently per participant from behavioral discrimination thresholds and cortical representational geometry, this model recovered an exact stimulus-space pre-image for all eight hues in both cases. That pre-image is the per-person correction filter, built from that individual's own cortical color geometry. We show, in the deutan case, that such a filter pulls the cortical hue geometry back toward the healthy-control reference; behavioral discrimination was restored, but not beyond a deployed accessibility filter.

\subsection{A geometric distortion of cortical color representation}
In both CVD participants, the deficit appeared as a structured distortion of cortical color geometry rather than a uniform attenuation of the chromatic signal. Representational geometry treats the pairwise distance structure among stimuli as an encoded relationship in each cortical region \cite{kriegeskorte2008, kriegeskorte2019}. Two complementary measurements expose a structural distortion, each at the stage where its signal exists. The representational dissimilarity matrix (RDM) shows \emph{which} pairwise distances deviate from the healthy-control (HC) geometry: disparity was significantly elevated at V2 in the deutan participant (Crawford \& Howell $p = 0.040$) and at V1 in the protan participant ($p = 0.007$). The two participants showed this elevated disparity at different areas (V2 and V1), not a common one. Leave-one-color-out decoding shows \emph{where} the continuous-hue manifold breaks down. Only hV4 supported above-chance interpolation in HC ($0.47 \pm 0.05$, $p = 0.044$), replicating \citeA{brouwer2009}. There, the protan participant fell significantly below HC ($0.13$, $p = 0.024$; exploratory single-case), and the deutan participant showed a marginal reduction in the same direction ($0.25$, $p = 0.082$). Both sat at zero adjacent accuracy, with a significant deficit at the S-cone intermediate blue ($d = 2.20$, $p = 0.042$). This account aligns with prior characterizations of CVD as a multidimensional deformation of color space rather than a uniform attenuation \cite{boehm2014, emery2021}.

\subsection{A neurally grounded, individualizable correction filter}
We built each correction filter by inverting that participant's fitted two-component model to its stimulus-space pre-image (Methods~\S\ref{sec:methods:filter}) --- the displayed hue that, after the cortex distorts it, is perceived as the intended color. Across the eight hues this filter applied a mean rotation of $|\delta\theta| = 26.3^\circ$ for the deutan participant and $16.2^\circ$ for the protan participant.

Fitting the behavioral and neural loss atoms independently revealed three benefits of grounding the correction in cortical geometry. First, the neural term recovered a confusion-axis direction the behavioral loss could not resolve: the protan behavioral-only fit did not beat the no-correction baseline ($\Delta L = +0.01$, 4 of 7 folds), whereas the RDM term resolved a clear rotation.
Second, it stabilized an already-informative solution: for the deutan participant the behavioral-only and combined fits shared the same argmin $(6^\circ, -42^\circ)$, and adding the neural term sharpened it without shifting it, lowering the boundary-saturation rate from $23\%$ to $9.3\%$.
Third, it tightened parameter stability in both participants, narrowing the resample interquartile range of the recovered parameters. The neural-only fit for the deutan participant independently returned the same direction ($\hat\beta_c = -26^\circ$), corroborating the behavioral estimate. A correction grounded in individual cortical geometry therefore differs in substance from a purely behavioral one.

The resulting filter is per-person and neurally grounded --- the architectural advance over a population-average correction. The two fitted filters differ by subtype in the \emph{direction} of the dominant confusion-axis term ($\hat\beta_c = -42^\circ$ for the deutan participant versus $+24^\circ$ for the protan participant). Per-axis magnitude is not identifiable (below) and is not the basis of the contrast. The architecture is individualizable by construction: each filter is built from one participant's measured cortical color geometry rather than from a fixed subtype-average spectral shift. Because $\theta_{\rm conf}$ is subtype-defined and the sample holds one deutan and one protan, this contrast is between-subtype. What makes each correction individual is that its distortion is read from that participant's own cortical color geometry, not assigned from their diagnostic subtype. Across held-out reference sets, the two-component mechanism class and the sign of the dominant confusion-axis term are stable --- the level the filter relies on. Their per-axis magnitudes are not separately identifiable (0 of 6 recovery checks survive FDR correction), so the between-subtype contrast rests on these robust directions rather than on absolute parameter values.

A population-average transform does not substitute for this per-person inverse, for a reason that holds independent of any fit. The Machado class --- a fixed spectral shift along the single confusion axis, even when scaled by a cortical gain \cite{machado2009} --- stays confined to that one axis and therefore does not represent distortion in orthogonal directions. For the protan participant it even collapses three distinct displayed hues onto a single pre-image near $127^\circ$, leaving no exact pre-image where that participant is most vulnerable (Appendix~\ref{app:altmodels}). Fitting the class confirmed the mismatch empirically --- its gain saturated near $g \approx 3$, implausibly implying overcompensation in observers who remain measurably impaired --- but the structural limit alone rules it out as an exact correction (Section~\ref{sec:methods:candidates}).

\subsection{Filter evaluation in the deutan case}
We tested the personalized filter against a deployed macOS accessibility filter in a second session in sub-08 (the protan was not collected). On cortical forward-tuning --- the leave-one-color-out geometry that was selectively impaired --- the personalized filter moved sub-08 toward the healthy-control reference in all four ROIs and outperformed the deployed filter, which instead distorted the forward map. Discrimination (LORO) was preserved under both filters, mirroring the discrimination--interpolation dissociation that defines the deficit: the population-average filter restores categorical decodability but not the continuous geometry, whereas the personalized filter restores both. Behaviorally, both filters improved discrimination from the baseline deficit to near-control levels --- both worked --- and the two could not be ranked against each other. On representational geometry, by contrast, the unfiltered baseline---not either filter---was closest to the healthy controls, in both SRM disparity and the shared-space RDM. Because the unfiltered condition presents the same physical colors the controls viewed, this proximity is the bar a filter must clear rather than evidence of correction. The personalized filter did not clear it: it reshaped the eight-hue geometry into a configuration unlike both the deutan baseline and the controls, and more compressed than either. This dissociation is coherent --- the filter restores the canonical angular ordering of hues that forward-tuning indexes while compressing the radial scale of the configuration --- and it bounds the geometry claim to forward-tuning. The personalized filter's distinctive advantage is thus confined to the pipeline-robust forward-tuning contrast, in a single deutan case; a behavioral advantage and generalization beyond this individual remain open.

\subsection{Limitations}
Four considerations bound the proof-of-concept scope of these findings. First, the CVD sample is $N = 2$. Single-case statistics \cite{crawford1998} permit individual-level inference, but population-level claims about deutan or protan subtypes require replication with more participants. As established above, the sign of $\hat\beta_c$ is the recoverable quantity. Whether the between-subtype $\hat\beta_c$ difference ($-42^\circ$ vs.\ $+24^\circ$) is subtype-level or idiosyncratic awaits power-adequate replication. Establishing per-person rather than subtype-average correction specifically requires testing several individuals \emph{within} a single subtype --- the decisive follow-up experiment. Second, the stimulus set was confined to a single isoluminant, iso-chroma locus ($L^{*} = 75$, chroma $= 40$). The filter is therefore derived and, in Phase~3, validated only along the hue circle at fixed lightness and saturation. It does not constrain how the correction generalizes to luminance- or saturation-varying stimuli or to naturalistic viewing --- gaps that denser stimulus sampling across these axes would close. Third, the HC reference cohort is small (seven controls), so the HC $\|\hat\beta\|$ leave-one-out distribution we report is a magnitude anchor for the CVD estimates, not a hypothesis test. A larger HC reference cohort would be required to arbitrate population-level distortion patterns. Fourth, we did not compute parameter-level bootstrap confidence intervals on $\hat\beta_s$ and $\hat\beta_c$, so the per-axis estimates are point values whose interval precision awaits the additional runs per participant that bootstrap resampling would require.

\subsection{Conclusion}
This work is a proof-of-concept that each individual's cortical color geometry, measured with fMRI, can ground a stimulus-space correction obtained by inverting a fitted model of that individual's representational distortion. It illustrates, in two cases, that a color-vision deficiency can present not as a uniform retinal loss to be undone by a fixed subtype-average spectral shift, but as an individually patterned, multidimensional distortion of the cortical color representation --- one structured enough to invert. The architecture that follows --- measure an individual's cortical geometry, fit its distortion, and invert it to a per-person correction --- is distinct by construction from population-average retinal approaches. A fixed subtype-average spectral shift does not represent a per-person distortion, whereas a per-person inverse does, for the mechanism class and up to the per-axis magnitude uncertainty quantified above.
In the deutan case, this cortical-level correction moved forward-tuning toward the healthy-control reference beyond a deployed filter; a behavioral advantage and broader validation remain to be established.
A correction spanning hue, lightness, and chroma jointly --- fitted from denser stimulus sampling, more runs per participant, and a larger HC reference pool --- is the path toward a deployable personalized filter. More broadly, treating a sensory deficit as an invertible distortion of its own cortical representation --- characterized and corrected one individual at a time --- offers a template for personalized, brain-based perceptual correction beyond color vision.
