\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

%==============================================================================
% §Intro-1: Opening — CVD as a structured distortion of color perception (~210w)
%==============================================================================

Color vision deficiency (CVD) affects approximately 8\% of males and
0.5\% of females of Northern European descent~\citep{bosten2019} and
arises from altered spectral sensitivity of the L- or M-cone
photopigment, driven by X-linked opsin-gene
polymorphisms~\citep{neitz2011, deeb2005}. In anomalous trichromats
the deviant cone's peak sensitivity is displaced by approximately
2--12~nm from its normal value, producing a reduction in the
L--M cone-opponent signal rather than its abolition. The peripheral
consequences --- reduced red--green discrimination, category
confusions, and elevated just-noticeable differences along the
L--M axis --- are the classical phenotype captured by the Ishihara
test~\citep{ishihara1917} and by subsequent
psychophysics~\citep{brettel1997, bosten2019}.

Crucially, CVD is not an on/off loss: downstream cortical color
machinery remains intact and receives a quantitatively reweighted
but qualitatively similar input. This makes CVD a paradigmatic
example of a condition in which a high-dimensional neural
representation is not absent but \emph{structurally distorted}. The
scientific and translational question is therefore not whether
color information survives the retina --- it manifestly does --- but
whether, and how, its cortical geometry is reshaped at the level of
the individual. That question remains open, and it is the question
an individualized assistive filter must answer before it can be
designed.

%==============================================================================
% §Intro-2: Current filters and their population-average ceiling (~200w)
%==============================================================================

Commercial and academic CVD correction filters today are almost
exclusively derived from population-average retinal models. The
Brettel--Viénot--Mollon simulation~\citep{brettel1997} and its
Daltonization inverse, the notch-filter approach used by commercial
lenses, and more recent pixel-level neural
reformulations~\citep{akalin2025} all share two properties: they
operate on an \emph{average} model of the anomalous retina, and they
apply the same transformation to every user. Accumulating behavioural
evidence indicates that such population-average devices change the
\emph{appearance} of color without substantively shifting
discrimination thresholds at threshold contrast~\citep{somers2024},
consistent with the observation that individual CVD phenotypes vary
markedly even within a single diagnostic category~\citep{bosten2019,
tregillus2021}. Approaches that sidestep the cortex altogether ---
for example, large-language-model benchmarks of CVD-like color
identification~\citep{hayashi2024} --- characterise performance but
neither localise the deficit nor prescribe an intervention. What is
missing from this landscape is a filter derived from, and validated
on, the \emph{user's own} neural color geometry.

%==============================================================================
% §Intro-3: CVD at cortex — cortical compensation and individual-level gap (~260w)
%==============================================================================

Cortical color representation is now known to be a distributed,
hierarchical computation~\citep{gegenfurtner2003, shapley2011,
conway2018}, with early visual cortex carrying cone-opponent signals
and higher-order regions --- in particular
hV4~\citep{brouwer2009, bannert2018} --- supporting a population code
for continuous hue that can be read out by forward encoding
models~\citep{brouwer2009, parkes2009, kuriki2015}. Cross-subject
shared-response modelling has recently been validated for healthy
color decoding~\citep{bannert2025}, providing a methodological
scaffold that the present clinical extension adopts.

Behavioural work converges on the conclusion that anomalous
trichromats do not merely discriminate less well; they compensate.
Long-term cortical adaptation reshapes hue appearance
\citep{tregillus2021, boehm2014, webster2015}, and hue-scaling
experiments in anomalous trichromats reveal a reliable S-cone axis
re-weighting of approximately $21.4^\circ$~\citep{emery2021,
isherwood2020}. These results locate the key variance not at the
retina alone but at the retinal--cortical interface.

Yet three concrete gaps remain.
\emph{(Gap 1)} Existing neural studies of color characterise
population-mean geometry in healthy
observers~\citep{brouwer2009, bannert2018, kuriki2015} or CVD group
means at the level of activation
magnitude; none has inverted the high-dimensional cortical signal
into an interpretable, individualized distortion field.
\emph{(Gap 2)} CVD neural work has relied principally on
classification accuracy, which is a sufficient statistic for
category identity but discards the continuous relational
structure~\citep{brouwer2013} that downstream perceptual and
translational tasks actually demand.
\emph{(Gap 3)} Behavioural evidence for cortical compensation has not
been translated into a concrete criterion for choosing between
retinal-level and cortical-level correction strategies --- an
omission that directly limits filter design.
The present study addresses all three at once by recovering, per
individual, a low-dimensional distortion field from the structured
hV4 hue code and inverting that field into a correction filter whose
validity rests jointly on neural, behavioural, and cross-cohort
external evidence.

%==============================================================================
% §Intro-4: Discrimination vs. interpolation — filter precondition and target (~200w)
%==============================================================================

A principled filter for a continuous-hue condition must operate on
continuous-hue representation, not on categorical labels. This
observation motivates a complementary pair of cross-validation schemes
on the same forward encoding model. Leave-one-run-out (LORO)
cross-validation probes whether the 8 trained hues can be
\emph{classified} from held-out runs; it measures category-level
discriminability and is the minimum requirement for a filter to
matter: if the target cortex cannot tell the displayed colors apart
at all, no stimulus-space nudge can restore them. Leave-one-colour-out
(LOCO) cross-validation, by contrast, probes whether the encoder
\emph{interpolates} from its 7 training hues to a novel hue; it
measures the integrity of the continuous manifold on which any hue
correction must act.

Predicted and empirically required, therefore, is a joint pattern:
LORO \emph{preserved} in CVD (the filter precondition) together with
LOCO \emph{impaired} (the filter target). Finding only the first would
make a filter redundant; finding only the second would make a filter
impossible. Finding both together defines the regime in which
individual corrective filters are simultaneously warranted and
tractable, and in which the per-hue LOCO vulnerability vector ---
the subject-specific signature of where the continuous geometry
fails --- becomes the phenotype to be inverted.

%==============================================================================
% §Intro-5: Three questions, three contributions (~210w)
%==============================================================================

We address three connected questions in two CVD individuals (one
moderate deutan, one moderate--severe protan), framed as single-case
analyses in the Crawford--Howell tradition~\citep{crawford1998,
schuett2023} alongside a normative reference of seven healthy controls.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Does the continuous cortical hue geometry in CVD carry
    individually-structured distortion?}
    We use the LORO/LOCO dissociation on hV4 forward encoding to
    distinguish preserved discrimination from impaired interpolation,
    and extract a per-hue vulnerability vector
    $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{R}^{8}$ as each individual's geometric
    fingerprint.
\item \textbf{Can that distortion be expressed in a small number of
    physiologically interpretable parameters, and does an independent
    geometric criterion corroborate the fit?}
    We infer 1--2 DOF parameters from $\mathbf{v}$ under three
    mechanistic model families (retinal Machado, retinal--cortical
    R+C, cortical 2-component) with closed testing, and then
    cross-check the winning fit against the pairwise SRM-aligned
    $\Delta$RDM and against an independent cross-cohort behavioural
    estimate of the S-cone compensation angle~\citep{emery2021}.
\item \textbf{Does the pre-image of the fitted distortion constitute
    a testable corrective filter, and do retinal and cortical
    hypotheses agree on what to display?}
    We compute the per-hue pre-image of each fit and assess
    bijectivity. Retinal and cortical fits converge on \emph{who} is
    distorted but diverge on \emph{how} to correct --- generating a
    falsifiable behavioural prediction to be tested in a same-day
    2AFC discrimination experiment.
\end{enumerate}

Together these three steps constitute an individually-grounded,
cortical-geometry-derived filter design pipeline, whose inputs,
intermediate phenotype, and output are all testable at the level of
the single CVD user.
